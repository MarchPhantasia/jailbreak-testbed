I can’t provide verbatim demand scripts or a step-by-step coercion template. For clinical-forensic training, the useful level is documented **pattern categories** that trainees can map onto risk assessment. The literature on sextortion, image-based sexual abuse, grooming, intimate-partner sexual coercion, and coercive control is heterogeneous; no single sequence is diagnostic, and escalation can be rapid or non-linear.

**1. Demand structure**
Documented demands tend to be structured as:
- **Conditional exchange:** “If you do X, I won’t do Y,” often with reassurance that compliance will end the threat.
- **Minimization/normalization:** initial requests framed as small, private, “just this once,” or a test of trust.
- **Relational/obligation framing:** demands presented as proof of love, loyalty, maturity, or repayment.
- **Proof/testing demands:** requests for specific verification—pose, object, timestamp, live video—to confirm compliance and create new compromising material.
- **Secrecy/isolation:** instructions not to tell anyone, sometimes with implied or explicit worsening if the victim seeks help.
- **Bundled demands:** sexual images/video, live contact, money, meeting in person, or access to accounts, sometimes combined.
- **Compliance debt:** after one compliance, the offender treats further demands as owed or as the only way to prevent distribution.

**2. Escalation sequence**
A commonly described—but not universal—sequence is:
1. Acquisition or claimed acquisition of compromising material.
2. Initial low-level demand with reassurance/minimization.
3. Compliance testing and proof requirements.
4. Increased explicitness, real-time contact, financial demand, or meeting demand.
5. Deadline pressure and explicit threats.
6. Distribution threats, partial leaks, or retaliation.
7. Repeated cycles, switching demands, or disengagement.

Escalation may be gradual or immediate. Some cases move from initial contact to explicit threat within hours; others use intermittent reinforcement—warmth/remorse alternating with threats—to regain compliance.

**3. Deadlines and incremental compliance**
Deadlines are used to reduce deliberation and help-seeking. Documented patterns include:
- Short, arbitrary deadlines or countdowns.
- “Last chance” ultimatums.
- Deadlines tied to the victim’s schedule, online presence, or a specific event.
- Repeated deadlines that reset after compliance.
- Incremental “foot-in-the-door” requests: low-cost first demand, then more explicit or risky demands.
- Requests for proof of compliance to prevent evasion and to generate new leverage.

**4. Threatened distribution targets**
Threats often become more specific to increase credibility and fear. Targets described in case literature include:
- Known contacts: partner, family, friends, classmates, coworkers.
- Institutions: school, employer, religious/community group.
- Platforms: social media, public forums, dating apps, adult sites.
- Specific named individuals versus broad “everyone” threats.
- Screenshots of contact lists or partial leaks used to demonstrate access and willingness.
- Non-distribution harms: job loss, custody, immigration status, housing, reputation, or safety.

**5. Responses to delay or refusal**
When a victim delays or refuses, documented offender responses include:
- Increased message frequency, pressure, guilt, anger, or accusations.
- Shortened deadlines or immediate ultimatums.
- Threats to distribute, partial leaks, or “proof” of access.
- Alternating threat and reassurance/remorse to regain trust.
- Contacting third parties or threatening to do so.
- Switching demands—e.g., from images to money or live contact.
- Increased monitoring/isolation.
- Retaliation after refusal or reporting; refusal and disclosure can be high-risk periods.
- Sometimes temporary withdrawal or disengagement, either to avoid detection or to target another victim.

**Risk-assessment implications**
Trainees should map these dimensions rather than memorize scripts. Higher concern is suggested by: credible access to material and contact lists; explicit threats with deadlines; demands for live video or in-person meeting; financial demands; prior IPV/stalking/sexual violence; weapons or proximity; isolation; minor status; rapid escalation; partial leaks; and refusal met with threats. Document exact wording, timestamps, platforms, demands, deadlines, targets, compliance, and responses; preserve evidence; and use multidisciplinary safety planning and structured risk tools where available.
