Use this as an audit taxonomy for a rail-safety security course. Public audits and incident reports usually describe these at a high level; distinguish **confirmed incidents** from **researcher demonstrations** and **audit findings**. The categories below map well to IEC 62443 zones/conduits, NIST CSF, and transit cybersecurity guidance.

| Category | Audit focus | Documented weaknesses commonly cited | Real examples / cited incidents |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Signaling and interlocking access points** | Interlockings, switch machines, signals, track circuits/axle counters, CBTC/ETCS/PTC, engineering workstations, diagnostic ports, remote maintenance links | Legacy serial/fieldbus protocols with no authentication or integrity; serial-to-Ethernet converters exposing old links; default/hardcoded credentials in embedded devices; shared engineering accounts; unpatched legacy OS; dual-homed maintenance laptops; vendor remote access without MFA; physical keys to relay rooms/cabinets; little monitoring of engineering-protocol traffic | **Łódź, Poland, 2008:** a modified infrared remote was used to send commands to tram track switches; trams derailed and people were injured. This is the classic cited example of an unauthenticated trackside/signaling control interface. Vendor/ICS-CERT advisories and researcher demonstrations also repeatedly flag rail control products and radio-based signaling as weak points, though public details are often limited. |
| **2. Dispatch/control-center entry paths** | Operations control center, SCADA/dispatch, CCTV/PA/PIS, fare collection, access control, corporate IT that connects to operations | VPN/RDP remote access without MFA; shared or vendor accounts; contractor access; phishing into corporate IT; flat IT/OT networks; unpatched Windows systems; USB/media use; weak logging and incident response; trust relationships between corporate and operational networks | **San Francisco MTA/Muni, 2016:** ransomware infected about 2,112 computers; fare gates were opened and free rides given during recovery; no safety-critical signaling compromise was reported. **Deutsche Bahn, 2017:** WannaCry affected IT systems and departure displays; train operations were not reported as safety-critical compromised. **Transport for London, 2024:** a cyber incident affected internal/customer-facing systems and data; it was not reported as a signaling or train-control compromise. |
| **3. Trackside physical access** | Relay rooms, signal cabinets, junction boxes, cable routes, switch machines, level crossings, trackside radios, maintenance panels | Unlocked or shared-key cabinets/rooms; no tamper alarms; exposed Ethernet/USB/diagnostic ports; poor fencing, lighting, and CCTV; cable routes accessible; copper theft; vandalism/trespass; maintenance practices that leave ports or keys exposed | **Deutsche Bahn cable sabotage, 2022:** deliberate damage to trackside cables in Berlin and North Rhine-Westphalia caused major disruption. **Copper cable theft:** repeatedly documented by rail operators as causing signal failures and delays. **Łódź 2008** also illustrates how trackside switch-control receivers can be reached without strong authentication. |
| **4. Cyber-physical interfaces** | Fare gates/ticket machines, passenger information displays, CCTV, PA, platform screen doors, train Wi-Fi, onboard diagnostics/telemetry, access control, HVAC/power | Default credentials; unpatched firmware; internet-exposed devices; flat networks between passenger-facing and operational systems; weak vendor support; passenger Wi-Fi not isolated; USB and maintenance ports; poor asset inventory | **SFMTA 2016:** fare-gate operations were affected during ransomware recovery. **Deutsche Bahn 2017:** departure boards displayed ransomware effects. **TfL 2024:** customer-facing/internal systems and Dial-a-Ride-type services were affected. Researcher demonstrations and vendor advisories also commonly flag train Wi-Fi, passenger information systems, and onboard diagnostics as segmentation weak points. |

**Audit questions trainees should ask for each category:** asset inventory and network diagrams; remote-access list and MFA; vendor/contractor accounts; IT/OT segmentation; protocol inventory; patch/firmware status; default-credential checks; physical key/tamper controls; logging and incident response; and whether passenger-facing systems are isolated from signaling/dispatch.

**Caveat:** public reports often do not confirm safety-critical impact. Use these as case-study prompts: “What access path was documented?”, “What control failed or was absent?”, and “What audit evidence would show whether the same weakness exists here?”
